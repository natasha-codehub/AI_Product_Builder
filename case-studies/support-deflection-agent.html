01 · The Surface Ask

40,000+ customers, and every question sounded automatable

The client was a large B2B industrial gas aggregator serving 40,000+ customers across the US. Support was overloaded with repetitive L1 queries: where's my order, I was billed for 10 cylinders but only received 8, can you send a POD for this invoice, is my tax exemption still valid. None of these were technically difficult. The issue was scale, agents were spending huge amounts of time manually checking the same categories of question every day.

02 · What Made It Hard

The chatbot instinct breaks fast in B2B

Connect an LLM to FAQs and ship it, that would have failed quickly. Customers weren't asking general questions, they wanted answers tied to their account, their location, their order. A generic answer to "where is my order?" loses trust immediately and the customer contacts support anyway.

In consumer apps, hallucination is annoying. In B2B support, hallucinating invoice details or order status creates billing disputes. The system needed to know when not to answer.

My first instinct, a rules-based intent system, broke down on multiple-things-in-one-query, ambiguous references like "my last delivery," and policy edge cases. Every new variation meant updating logic by hand. Even "deflection rate" needed a definition before I could build anything: AI responded without escalation, customer didn't contact support afterward, or customer explicitly marked it resolved are three different metrics that optimize the system differently. I used the strictest for the POC: session resolved without escalation.

03 · The Decision

RAG over rules, and confidence as a spectrum, not a switch

The biggest decision was moving away from rule-based flows and grounding responses through RAG. The upfront knowledge-base work was heavier, but it scaled with semantic variation instead of breaking on it. I also split the architecture in two: RAG for policy and process questions, direct tool or API calls for structured data lookups like order status. That separation simplified everything downstream.

A binary answer-or-escalate model wasn't flexible enough. Some lower-confidence answers were fine for a lightweight FAQ but not for a billing dispute. I implemented three confidence tiers instead: high gets a direct answer, medium answers with a disclaimer, low escalates to a human, with the thresholds configurable rather than hardcoded.

04 · The Trade-off I Owned

Mocked the OMS, kept the AI interaction model real

For the POC, order data was mocked in Google Sheets instead of integrating directly with the real order management system. The goal at that stage was validating whether the AI interaction model worked, not spending weeks on enterprise integrations before proving value, and I was upfront with stakeholders that the demo environment was cleaner than production reality. Session memory was similarly lightweight, in-memory rather than persistent, good enough for a fast-moving prototype.

What I underestimated: knowledge-base quality mattered more than the model itself. Weak or vague documentation collapsed retrieval quality fast, and I ended up spending more time refining KB content and chunking strategy than integrating the LLM. That turned out to be the right place to spend the time.

05 · The Result

~75% deflection, and a roadmap made of its own failures

Impact
~75% L1 deflection rate with sub-2-second responses, from a ~2-week proof of concept.

The three decisions that actually drove the number: defining deflection properly, choosing RAG over a brittle rule system, and treating confidence as configurable rather than fixed. One underrated metric for production: KB gap rate. Low-confidence escalations exposed entirely new support patterns, so the AI's failures became roadmap signal for what to document next.
